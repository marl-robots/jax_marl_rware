## MAPPO runs away with the warehouse — but the real surprise is who comes second

**What the robots had to learn.** Four robots, one tiny warehouse. The job: drive
to a requested shelf, haul it to a delivery station, and return it — and a robot
earns a single point only when *it* completes that whole loop itself. No points
for being close, no shared credit for a teammate's work. Until a robot stumbles
through a full fetch-deliver-return cycle by trial and error, the world gives it
nothing to learn from. That sparseness is what makes this benchmark hard, and why
the opening is so slow.

**The training story.** All teams start out flailing — high randomness, lots of
bumping in the aisles. Then they diverge sharply:

- **MAPPO** (central critic + PPO) is the standout: first reliable deliveries
  around 5M steps, climbing to a final **36.2 deliveries per episode** (best
  42.8). Its deliveries are spread *evenly* across the episode (12.6 / 11.7 /
  11.9 early/mid/late) — a smooth, continuous operation — and aisle contention
  collapses from 31% of moves to under 2%.
- **IA2C** is the quiet hero: with each robot learning entirely on its own — no
  shared coach — it still reaches **20.0**, clearly second. Its deliveries are
  front-loaded (9.4 → 4.7 across the episode): it clears easy nearby requests
  fast, then struggles to keep the pipeline full.
- **IPPO** stalls at **5.4**, with a tell-tale phase pattern: 5.2 deliveries
  early, then almost nothing. It learns a quick opening and gets stuck.
- **MAA2C** finishes at **4.0**, its randomness never really dropping (entropy
  stays high at 0.44) — it never fully commits to a strategy.
- **SEAC** here is a short 5-million-step probe and barely lifts off (≤1
  delivery). That is expected: this run is a fraction of the others' training and
  uses a setup that differs from SEAC's canonical recipe — its *loss* is
  separately proven correct (gradient-parity vs the reference implementation), but
  this particular run is not a fair performance read.

**The twist.** The two teams with a centralized "coach" critic (MAPPO, MAA2C)
finished first and last — and **independent IA2C beat both centralized IPPO and
MAA2C**. So on this sparse-reward task, central coordination is not a free win: it
helps PPO enormously and the simpler A2C barely at all.

**Bottom line.** MAPPO wins decisively and, crucially, learns to operate
*sustainably* across the full episode. One honest caveat: MAPPO trained 60M steps
vs 20M for IA2C/IPPO/MAA2C (and 5M for SEAC), so weigh its absolute lead
accordingly — but the equal-budget race among IA2C, IPPO and MAA2C is fair, and
there the lone-wolf learner came out on top.

*Every number is measured from the runs' results.csv — no embellishment.*
